## 1. Where the formula comes from

BM25 is three ideas multiplied together.

**(a) Rare terms matter more.** `idf(t) = log(1 + (N − df + 0.5)/(df + 0.5))`.
A term in 3 of 50,000 documents scores ~9.7; a term in 25,000 scores ~0.69. The `+0.5`
terms are smoothing (they keep `df = 0` and `df = N` finite), and the outer `1 +` keeps
IDF positive. The classic Robertson form omits the `1 +` and goes **negative** for terms
in more than half the corpus, which means a common word actively *subtracts* score — an
occasionally useful property, and a confusing one when debugging. We use the positive form.

**(b) Repetition saturates.** Raw term frequency is wrong: a document mentioning
"người lao động" ten times is not ten times more relevant. The factor

```
tf·(k1 + 1) / (tf + k1)
```

rises steeply at first and then flattens toward `k1 + 1`. With `k1 = 1.2`: tf=1 → 1.0,
tf=2 → 1.47, tf=5 → 1.77, tf=20 → 2.10. The fifth mention adds almost nothing.

`k1 = 0` collapses this to 1.0 for any tf ≥ 1 — pure binary presence.

**(c) Long documents are discounted.** Replace `k1` in the denominator with
`k1·(1 − b + b·|d|/avgdl)`. At `b = 1` a document twice the average length has its
saturation constant doubled, which pushes its term contributions down. At `b = 0` the
correction vanishes.

## 2. The `b` argument, properly

Long documents match more queries **by accident** — they contain more distinct terms, so
some query term lands somewhere. Length normalisation corrects for that. But length is not
*purely* noise: a longer document may genuinely cover more, and be genuinely more likely to
contain the answer.

So the right `b` depends on **how much of your length variation is accidental**:

| Situation | Length variation is | Best `b` |
|---|---|---|
| whole văn bản indexed (a Bộ luật next to a two-page Thông tư) | mostly accidental — a Bộ luật is not "more relevant", it is just bigger | high, 0.75–1.0 |
| điều-level chunks (comparable units) | mostly informative — a long điều really does cover more | lower, 0.3–0.5 |
| khoản-level (very short, uniform) | little variation either way | `b` barely matters; expect a flat grid |

**This is why chunking and `b` must be tuned together, not separately.** The grid search
script takes `--corpus`, so run it once per granularity.

## 3. Reading a flat grid

If all 16 cells are within noise of each other, that is information, not a failure:

- Your corpus units are length-uniform (so `b` is inert) **and** query terms rarely repeat
  in documents (so `k1` is inert). Common at khoản granularity.
- Or your queries are matching on one or two very high-IDF terms — the decree number — and
  the IDF term dominates everything else.

Either way: **stop tuning BM25 and move to Phase 2.** Record the flat grid as a result.
"BM25 hyperparameters were inert on this corpus, so gains had to come from elsewhere" is a
legitimate and useful paper sentence.

## 4. Tokenisation decisions and what they cost

**Keeping `/`, `.`, `-` inside tokens.** `100/2019/nđ-cp` split into `100`, `2019`, `nđ`,
`cp` becomes four low-IDF tokens matching thousands of documents. Kept whole, it is a
near-unique maximum-IDF term identifying one document. In a corpus where queries quote
decree numbers, this single decision is worth more than the entire `k1`/`b` grid.

**Segmentation is a genuine trade-off for BM25** (unlike for PhoBERT models, where it is
mandatory):

| | unsegmented (syllables) | segmented (`học_sinh`) |
|---|---|---|
| vocabulary | small, dense postings | large, sparse postings |
| precision of a match | lower — `học` matches many compounds | higher |
| robustness | high — no segmenter to get it wrong | lower — one segmenter error kills the match |
| behaviour on rare terms | degrades gracefully | can fail completely |

Measure it. Phase 1 Task B2 runs both.

**Stopwords: off by default here.** IDF already discounts them, and legal phrasing leans on
function words ("theo quy định của"). Removing them can break a phrase match and rarely
helps. Turn them on only if dev says so.

## 5. Why the index takes k1/b at scoring time

`rank_bm25` bakes the parameters into the constructor, so a 16-point grid means 16 index
builds — and tokenising a large corpus is the expensive part. `src/bm25.BM25Index` stores
postings `(doc_idx, tf)` plus `doc_len`, and applies `k1`/`b` in `score()`. One build, 16
scorings. On a 50k-document corpus that is minutes instead of an hour.

The scoring loop is over *query terms*, not documents: for each term, look up its posting
list and add its contribution to just those documents. A query of 12 terms touches only the
documents containing those terms — typically a small fraction of the corpus. That is why
sparse retrieval is fast, and it is the same reason a cross-encoder cannot be.

## 6. Known BM25 failure modes in legal IR

| Failure | Why | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|
| paraphrase ("nghỉ phép năm" vs "nghỉ hằng năm") | no shared term, score 0 | dense retrieval, then fuse |
| near-miss numbers (Điều 12 vs Điều 112) | both are single tokens; IDF cannot tell you which you meant | reranker; exact-match features |
| very short queries | few terms, so one spurious match dominates | `min_k`, fusion |
| negated conditions | BM25 is a bag of words; "không áp dụng" scores like "áp dụng" | cross-encoder — this is the clearest case for one |
| a query quoting a whole clause | length effects swamp IDF | length-normalise queries too, or truncate |

Categories 1, 2 and 4 map directly onto the error-analysis labels in
`tools/error_analysis.py`. That is not a coincidence — the taxonomy was built from them.

## Check yourself

1. Your grid's best cell is `k1=0.9, b=1.0` at document granularity and `k1=1.5, b=0.3` at
   article granularity. Is that contradictory?
2. A query is one decree number and nothing else. Which BM25 parameter matters most, and why?
3. You segment the corpus but forget to segment the queries. What happens to the score, and
   does anything raise an error?

<details><summary>answers</summary>

1. **No — it is exactly what the theory predicts.** At document level, length variation is
   accidental (a Bộ luật is not more relevant for being long), so heavy normalisation
   `b=1.0` helps. At article level, units are comparable and residual length is informative,
   so light normalisation `b=0.3` helps. The `k1` shift is secondary: longer documents
   repeat terms more, so saturation matters more at document level (lower `k1`).
2. **None of them — IDF dominates.** `k1` scales term repetition and the number appears once;
   `b` scales length and the query has no length to speak of. The score is essentially
   `idf(decree_number)`, which is huge. This is why BM25 is unbeatable on identifier queries
   and why you must not let a dense model outvote it in fusion on those.
3. Corpus tokens are `học_sinh`; query tokens are `học`, `sinh`. **Neither appears in the
   other's vocabulary**, so most query terms match nothing and scores collapse toward zero
   — recall craters. **Nothing raises an error.** It looks like a bad model. This is the
   single most common silent preprocessing bug, and it is why `src/normalize.tokenize` takes
   the segmenter as an argument used identically on both sides.
</details>
